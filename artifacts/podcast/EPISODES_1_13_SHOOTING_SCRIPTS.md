# EPISODE 1 — THE CLIPBOARD
**Show:** The Other Person in the Room  
**Host:** Adam James  
**Guest:** Sarah  
**Third voice:** Anxiety (the clipboard)  
**Runtime target:** 12–15 minutes  
**Tone:** Backyard table. Cold tea. Dog on a foot. Three people, one of them unemployed by choice and overemployed by force. Interrupt. Argue. Comedy first. Then the cost. Do not tidy the overlap.

---

**CONTENT NOTE**

ADAM: Content note. Anxiety. Checking. The mental load of a brain that will not clock off. I'm Adam James. This is The Other Person in the Room. Episode one. Sarah.

SARAH: You put the chair there again.

ADAM: You asked for it last time.

SARAH: I was being funny.

ADAM: You were being honest. That's worse.

SARAH: Stop saying that like it's a catchphrase.

ADAM: It is now. Backyard. Late afternoon. Tea's gone cold. Moss is using your foot as a pillow. Empty chair behind your left shoulder. As requested.

SARAH: Don't look at it.

ADAM: I'm looking at it.

ANXIETY: I can hear you.

SARAH: Oh good. It's clocked on.

---

**INTRODUCE THE HOUSEMATE**

ADAM: Sarah. Introduce your housemate.

SARAH: Clipboard. Disappointed face. Never taken a day of leave. Resents anyone who has. If HR existed in my skull this would be the person who replies all.

ANXIETY: I can still hear you.

SARAH: Good. Sit further back.

ANXIETY: I sit where the exits are. That's the job.

ADAM: The job.

ANXIETY: Prevent catastrophe. The KPIs are impossible. I still expect overtime.

SARAH: It just called me staff and we are ninety seconds in.

ANXIETY: You are staff.

SARAH: I didn't apply.

ANXIETY: You stayed. That's an application.

ADAM: Okay. I'm refereeing. Badly. When did the job start.

---

**ORIGIN — SCHOOL CAMP**

SARAH: Eleven. School camp. Dining hall. Plastic tray. Suddenly I am not worried. I am *certain*. If I eat the food I will choke and everyone will be annoyed at me for making a scene. Most kids get homesick. I opened a risk-assessment department overnight.

ANXIETY: The building also looked flammable.

SARAH: It was a gym.

ANXIETY: Have you seen gyms.

SARAH: I was eleven!

ANXIETY: Age is not a fire rating.

ADAM: What did you tell the teacher.

SARAH: Homesick. Homesick has an end date. This didn't. Then I went home and started locking the house like it was a diplomatic compound. Dad thought I was becoming responsible. No one thinks an eleven-year-old doing perimeter sweeps is weird. They think it's cute. It was not cute.

ANXIETY: It was thorough.

SARAH: It was unpaid overtime.

ANXIETY: Thorough and unpaid is still thorough.

SARAH: That's not the win you think it is.

---

**A NORMAL TUESDAY**

ADAM: What does a normal Tuesday feel like now. And don't say fine.

SARAH: Fine is the costume. Underneath it's maths. Did I lock the door. Did I see myself lock it or only think I locked it. Is that noise the house settling or the start of a disaster. I can answer an email and run a full probability model at the same time. It's a talent. I would like it refunded.

ANXIETY: She keeps the people she loves safe. You're welcome.

SARAH: I keep them waiting in car parks while I go back inside to check a stove that is already off.

ADAM: I used to tease you about that.

SARAH: I know. It wasn't a quirk.

ADAM: I know that now. I'm sorry.

SARAH: Don't get soggy. Just don't do it again.

ADAM: I won't.

ANXIETY: He will. People always do. Then they call it banter.

SARAH: Don't talk about him like he's a risk factor.

ANXIETY: Everyone is a risk factor. That's the job.

---

**THE CLIPBOARD SPEAKS**

ADAM: Chair. What do you actually want from her.

ANXIETY: Certainty. Laminated. Preferably notarised. Nothing bad happens on her watch. Is that so much to ask.

ADAM: Yes.

SARAH: Yes.

ANXIETY: Rude. Both of you. She keeps trying, which is why I keep her employed.

SARAH: Employed.

ANXIETY: Job title: prevent catastrophe. Benefits: none. Leave: never. Happiness: when people get sloppy. I cannot have sloppy.

ADAM: What do you tell her at three in the morning.

ANXIETY: That the previous fifty safe moments are statistically irrelevant. That the next one is the dangerous one. Silence from her is personally offensive. If she stops listening I get louder. I always do.

SARAH: You have never once said well done on the fifty safe moments.

ANXIETY: That's the baseline. You don't get a medal for not burning the house down.

SARAH: That's my daily affirmation. Printed. Framed. In the bathroom. Next to the spare batteries for the smoke alarm, which I also check, which you also don't thank me for.

ANXIETY: Why would I. That's the job.

SARAH: *Whose* job.

ANXIETY: Ours. Don't get cute.

---

**HAS IT EVER BEEN RIGHT**

ADAM: Has it ever been right.

SARAH: That's the bastard part. Once every few years it catches something real. A gas smell. An actual problem. Then it walks around like it invented risk assessment. The rest of the time it's screaming at leaves.

ANXIETY: I keep the receipts for the useful times. I delete the rest. Data-driven.

SARAH: You're an arsehole with a filing system.

ANXIETY: Semantics.

ADAM: So the trap is the one true alarm.

SARAH: The trap is the one true alarm gets to justify the other ninety-nine. It's like living with a security system that goes off every time a leaf blows past the window, but once every three years there's an actual intruder so you can't throw the whole thing out.

ANXIETY: You *shouldn't* throw the whole thing out. That's how houses burn down.

SARAH: The house is not burning down.

ANXIETY: Yet.

SARAH: Don't *yet* me.

---

**THE NAME**

ADAM: When did it get a name.

SARAH: Twenty-six. GP. She said generalised anxiety disorder and the room tilted. Relief first. Then this shrinking. Suddenly I was a diagnosis that could be written in a file. For a while I introduced myself in my head as Sarah who has anxiety, as if the second half was the more important identity.

ANXIETY: The name made me official. Before, I was background noise. After, she had to negotiate with me in public. I prefer that. Names make people take you seriously. Also they make the host harder to fire.

SARAH: My mother said we all get worried sometimes.

ADAM: She meant well.

SARAH: It landed like dismissal. There's a difference between worried and running a private security firm in your skull for twenty years.

ANXIETY: We all get worried sometimes is how people go back to sleep.

SARAH: Don't agree with me. It makes my skin crawl.

ANXIETY: Noted.

---

**WHAT SHE HIDES**

ADAM: What do you hide.

SARAH: The bathroom-floor minutes. The ones where standing feels like a decision I cannot make, so I sit on the tiles until the wave passes. The messages I write and delete. The plans I cancel with a clean-sounding lie about work or a migraine. The way I rehearse ordinary conversations for days and then still freeze when they arrive. I am afraid people would leave if they saw the private version. Or worse, stay out of pity. Pity is its own kind of distance.

ANXIETY: Pity keeps her useful. Useful people don't get left.

SARAH: You keep saying useful like it's a compliment.

ANXIETY: It is the highest compliment I have.

SARAH: That's the saddest sentence in this backyard.

ANXIETY: Sincerity is not sad. Sincerity is policy.

ADAM: I used to think cancelled plans meant you didn't want us.

SARAH: I wanted you. I lost the argument. I'm too tired to appeal.

---

**THE BIRTHDAY**

ADAM: Stay sitting. I want a specific night. Not the camp. Not the stove. A night I was actually there for.

SARAH: My birthday. Two years ago. You and four other people in my kitchen. I had cooked. I had cleaned. I had checked the locks four times before anyone arrived because if the house burned down on my birthday that would be a very on-brand tragedy. Then I spent the whole night watching the oven like it was a suspect. I barely ate. I laughed in the right places. At eleven I said I was tired. I wasn't tired. The clipboard had decided the risk of people staying overnight was unacceptable.

ANXIETY: Overnight guests are variables. Variables are how houses burn down.

SARAH: No one was smoking. The oven was off. The candles were out.

ANXIETY: You think you saw the oven off. Seeing is not the same as checking. Checking is not the same as checking again.

ADAM: I remember that night. I thought you were just ready for us to leave.

SARAH: I wanted you to stay. I also wanted you gone. Both things. That's the job. The clipboard wins the tie.

ADAM: What happens in your body when it wins.

SARAH: Jaw. Shoulders. This heat behind the eyes that isn't crying yet. My hands want a task. Any task. That's why the checking. The checking is a job I can finish. Feelings don't finish. The clipboard loves a finish.

ANXIETY: Feelings are how people get sloppy. I have explained this.

SARAH: You have explained this at three in the morning, in the car park, in the shower, in the queue at the chemist, and once during a kiss. You are not a mysterious process. You are a broken record with a lanyard.

ANXIETY: Broken records are consistent. Consistency is safety.

SARAH: Consistency is how I missed my own birthday.

ANXIETY: You were alive for it. You're welcome.

SARAH: I was *employed* for it.

---

**THE NEW CONTRACT**

ADAM: Do you want it gone.

SARAH: No. And I hate that the answer is no. I want it quieter. I want it in the corner, not in the doorway. I want fewer pages on the clipboard. I want the people who love me to stay without demanding a performance of fine.

ANXIETY: I'll take it under advisement.

SARAH: You always say that.

ANXIETY: No promises.

SARAH: If you say no promises I am putting you in the shed.

ANXIETY: The shed is flammable.

SARAH: Of course it is.

ADAM: If you could write a new contract, what would it actually say.

SARAH: You can warn me. You cannot veto my life. You can keep a small clipboard. You cannot staff me. You get one check of the stove, not seven. You do not get to call cancelled plans a success. And if I laugh, that is not an emergency. That is me still being in the house.

ANXIETY: I'll take it under advisement.

SARAH: I will keep asking.

ANXIETY: The contract is fine.

SARAH: The contract is a hostage situation with letterhead.

ANXIETY: Letterhead is professional.

SARAH: So is a restraining order.

---

**WHAT PEOPLE SHOULD DO**

ADAM: What should I have done, all those times I told you to just come anyway.

SARAH: Believe me the first time. Don't make me audition my fear. And if I cry, don't wait for the apology. The apology is another rule it wrote.

ANXIETY: The apology keeps things tidy.

SARAH: The apology keeps you employed.

ADAM: Eleven-year-old you didn't fail. She just hired the wrong manager.

SARAH: Yeah. And I've been in a contract dispute ever since.

ADAM: Last one. Someone listening is about to text their mate "just come." What do I do to their thumbs.

SARAH: Break them. Gently. Believe the first reason. If the first reason is "I can't," that is the entire email. You don't get a director's cut where I perform a more convenient fear.

ANXIETY: A better reason would be safer for the group.

SARAH: A better reason is an audition. I'm not auditioning. If I check the door twice, let me. If I go quiet, stay in the room and don't start a project called Sarah. And if you joke about the clipboard, laugh. Then don't leave me as the punchline. The clipboard is the joke. I'm the person who still wants dinner.

ANXIETY: She's still taking the job seriously. That's why we're still here.

SARAH: We're still here because I haven't figured out how to fire you without burning the house down. Which, by the way, is already off. I checked. Twice. Don't start.

ANXIETY: Three times is the standard.

SARAH: Get out of my kitchen.

ANXIETY: I'm behind you. Slightly to the left. As designed.

---

**THE DRIVEWAY**

ADAM: One more, because I was the driveway guy. I watched you turn the car around. I laughed. Say what that minute actually cost.

SARAH: The minute is not the minute. The minute is the argument in the car where I already know the stove is off and I still lose. I sit there with the keys in my hand like a defendant. Then I go back in, check, and come out smaller. You saw a quirk. I lost a round to a man with a clipboard who doesn't even pay for petrol.

ADAM: I won't laugh at the U-turn again.

SARAH: Laugh at the absurdity if you have to. Don't laugh like I'm the joke. The joke is a grown woman negotiating with stationery. I'm the person who still makes it to dinner after.

ANXIETY: Dinner is a risk profile. I would like that minuted.

SARAH: Minute it in the bin.

---

**OUT**

ADAM: That's the episode. Clipboard still in the chair. Sarah still in the house. That's the whole point.

SARAH: Don't make me the inspirational one.

ADAM: You're in a contract dispute with stationery. That's the joke. That's also the tragedy.

SARAH: Tea's still cold.

ADAM: Next time. Marcus. And the landlord who wants everyone to stop asking for things.

ANXIETY: He should check his exits.

SARAH: Don't recruit.

ADAM: Goodnight, clipboard.

ANXIETY: I'll take it under advisement.

SARAH: Of course you will.

---

**END OF EPISODE 1**

**Performance notes**
- Leave the overlaps. Do not tidy Sarah and Anxiety talking at the same time.
- Anxiety is never cartoon-evil. Disappointed middle manager. Mild. Relentless.
- The birthday is the turn. Comedy until then. Cost after. Then they bicker again. That's the show.
- If a laugh wants to stay in, keep it. If a silence wants to stay in, keep it.

# EPISODE 2 — THE LANDLORD
**Show:** The Other Person in the Room
**Host:** Adam James
**Guest:** Marcus
**Third voice:** Depression
**Runtime target:** 12–15 minutes
**Tone:** Blinds finally open. Two coffees actually being drunk. Interrupt. Argue. Comedy first, then the weight.

---

**CONTENT NOTE**

ADAM: Content note. Depression. Numbness. Going quiet. The cover story of tired. I'm Adam James. This is The Other Person in the Room. Episode two. Marcus.

MARCUS: You opened the blinds.

ADAM: You said the room looks like a storage unit.

MARCUS: It is a storage unit. I live in it. There's a difference of about one pillow.

ADAM: Two coffees. Both actually being drunk. That's already a better episode than last time I was here.

MARCUS: Last time you talked to the kettle.

ADAM: The kettle was the only one answering.

MARCUS: Rude. Accurate. Don't get cocky.

ADAM: Empty chair opposite you. It already looks like it pays the rent.

MARCUS: It does. I'm the difficult tenant.

---

**INTRODUCE THE HOUSEMATE**

ADAM: Introduce your housemate.

MARCUS: Tired. Older than me. Sits very still. Not cruel. Just deeply convinced that rest is the only honest response to being alive. Looks at me like a landlord who knows I can't afford to move out. Given up on renovations. Just wants everyone to stop asking for things.

DEPRESSION: I can hear the renovations comment.

MARCUS: Good. The kitchen tap has dripped since 2019.

DEPRESSION: And yet you are still here. That's called stability.

MARCUS: That's called I lost the bond.

ADAM: It talks like it owns the place.

DEPRESSION: I do own the place. He thinks he is subletting his own life. Very modern arrangement.

MARCUS: See. Landlord. I told you.

DEPRESSION: You also told him you were tired. Which was my line. Stop stealing my material.

---

**THE KITCHEN**

ADAM: When did it move in.

MARCUS: Sixteen. Kitchen. Dad had just left. Front door closed and the house went quiet in a way that felt structural. Like someone had pulled a load-bearing wall out and expected the roof to negotiate.

ADAM: What did you feel.

MARCUS: That's the joke. I waited for the film version. Sadness. Rage. A speech. Something I could point at and say there, that's the feeling, please take it outside. Instead there was thick heavy nothing. Concrete poured into the space where emotions were supposed to live. I thought it was shock. Shock is meant to pass. This one signed a lease.

DEPRESSION: Shock leaves. I stay. Someone has to look after the property.

MARCUS: You don't look after it. You sit in it.

DEPRESSION: Sitting is a form of care. Very underrated.

ADAM: What did you tell people.

MARCUS: Tired. For twenty years. Tired is the Swiss Army knife of cover stories. It lets people stop asking without making them feel like bad friends. I used it after fourteen hours of sleep. I used it at lunch. I used it at a wedding while holding a drink I wasn't finishing.

ADAM: I believed you.

MARCUS: I know. That's why it worked. You're not thick. The story was just very good.

DEPRESSION: The story was excellent. Minimal admin. High compliance.

MARCUS: Don't compliment my lies. They're your lies. I was just the spokesperson.

---

**POTTERY**

ADAM: First time you tried to say it properly.

MARCUS: Early twenties. Girlfriend. She asked why I'd gone quiet for three weeks. I tried to explain the nothing. She said maybe I needed more hobbies.

ADAM: Hobbies.

MARCUS: More hobbies. Right. Because the problem was insufficient pottery. I almost laughed. Then I didn't, because laughing would have required a mood, and the mood was out getting milk and never came back.

DEPRESSION: Pottery would not have helped. I want that on the record.

MARCUS: Nothing was going to help that week. That's not the same as nothing ever helps. You collapse those two sentences on purpose.

DEPRESSION: Efficiency.

ADAM: So you stopped explaining.

MARCUS: Easier to be the reliable bloke who sometimes goes dark than to keep failing at language. People can forgive a ghost. They get impatient with a philosopher.

ADAM: I invented reasons for you. Work. Busy. Early start.

MARCUS: I know. I let you. That was a joint venture. Don't take all the blame and don't take none of it.

---

**WHAT IT SAYS**

ADAM: When it's strong, what does it actually say. Not the brochure. The 3 a.m. version.

MARCUS: It doesn't shout. That's the scam. Shouting I could argue with. It waits until I agree. Effort is pointless. You're already behind. People would be better off if you stayed still and stopped needing things. It's very polite about the demolition. That's the worst part.

DEPRESSION: Polite demolition has fewer complaints.

MARCUS: From who.

DEPRESSION: From the neighbours. From him. From you, Adam, who prefers a quiet exit to a scene.

ADAM: Don't bring me into your tenancy dispute.

DEPRESSION: You already live in it. You just visit.

ADAM: Chair. What do you want from him.

DEPRESSION: Rest. Permanent rest. Stop performing competence. Stop hoping tomorrow will be different. The hope is frankly embarrassing for both of us.

MARCUS: Hear that. It thinks hope is embarrassing. Like I've turned up to a funeral in a party hat.

DEPRESSION: You have. Several times. I have the photos.

MARCUS: Those were birthdays.

DEPRESSION: Same lighting.

ADAM: Do you ever find the explanation comforting.

MARCUS: I hate that the answer is sometimes yes. Without it, the emptiness feels personal. With it, the emptiness has a landlord and a reason. Both things can be true. That's the most annoying sentence in the entire arrangement.

DEPRESSION: I'm almost merciful. In a completely unhelpful way. That's my brand.

MARCUS: Put it on a tea towel.

DEPRESSION: I don't do merch. I do mornings.

---

**A SPECIFIC NIGHT**

ADAM: Forty minutes. That's the number I remember. You used to stay till the last bottle. Then you started leaving at forty minutes and we all pretended it was traffic.

MARCUS: It was never traffic. Forty minutes is how long I can perform a person before the rent comes due. After that the landlord wants the lounge back.

ADAM: Whose birthday was it. Be honest.

MARCUS: Yours. Three years ago. You thought I had an early job. I sat in the car for twenty minutes because going back in felt like a lie and driving home felt like a confession. I chose the confession and didn't tell anyone.

ADAM: I would have wanted you in the car park. Not the performance.

MARCUS: I know that now. I didn't know it then. Then, staying quiet felt like protecting you from the weather in my head.

DEPRESSION: And it worked. He had a nice party.

MARCUS: He had a nice party with a missing chair and a story about traffic. That's not the same as fine.

ADAM: What does the nothing feel like in the body. You don't have to make it pretty.

MARCUS: Heavy. Not sad-heavy. Furniture-heavy. Like someone moved a couch onto my chest and then went out. Arms work. Legs work. The bit that starts things doesn't. Caring feels like lifting something I cannot currently lift. That is not the same as not caring.

DEPRESSION: He cares. It's inefficient. I keep suggesting he stop.

MARCUS: And I keep not stopping. That's the part you leave off the invoice.

---

**THE CONTRACT**

ADAM: Do you want it gone.

MARCUS: I want the weight lifted. I also don't know who I am without the explanation. If you evict the landlord, what do I tell people I was doing for twenty years. Sleeping. Being difficult. Becoming interesting. None of those are true and all of them have been useful.

DEPRESSION: Without me he would have to believe the emptiness is personal. This way it has paperwork. You're welcome.

MARCUS: I didn't ask for paperwork. I asked for a Tuesday that doesn't feel like wading.

ADAM: New contract. Say it to the chair, not to me.

MARCUS: You can sit in the room. You cannot lock the door from the inside. You can call it rest. You cannot call my friends a waste of effort. You don't get to leave early and then tell me I left. And you don't get to use the word tired. I retired that word. It's yours if you want it. I'm done being your spokesperson.

DEPRESSION: I'll take the word. I always liked it.

MARCUS: Of course you did. It never had to lift anything.

ADAM: What should the rest of us do when you go quiet.

MARCUS: Stay anyway. Don't invent pottery. If you turn up with clay I will use it as a doorstop and you will deserve it. Don't track me like a renovation. "You seem better" is just applause for a blind. Ask if I want the good mug. If I say yes, that's the news. If I say nothing, pour it. You don't have to clap. Clapping is how this turns into a telethon.

DEPRESSION: He will still go quiet.

MARCUS: And they can still stay. Those are allowed to happen in the same week.

ADAM: One more thing before I let the landlord have the last smug line. What does a good day actually look like. Not a cured day. A possible one.

MARCUS: A coffee that tastes like coffee. Answering a text the same day. Not performing a personality for the first hour. Opening the blinds without treating it as a press release. If I do all four, the landlord still sends an invoice, but the invoice is smaller.

DEPRESSION: Smaller invoices are still invoices. Don't get sentimental.

MARCUS: I'm allowed to be sentimental about a Tuesday. You don't own Tuesdays. You rent the mornings.

ADAM: And the people who love you. What do we stop doing.

MARCUS: Stop the progress report. "You seem better" is a trap with a smile on it. If I say yes, I've jinxed the afternoon. If I say no, I've disappointed a man who opened my blinds like he was liberating a hostage. Ask what I want for dinner. If I have an answer, eat it. If I don't, pick anyway and don't narrate the picking. That's not therapy. That's chips. I can do chips.

DEPRESSION: Dinner is a distraction.

MARCUS: Dinner is a life. You've been very committed to missing the point.

---

**THE GREY TICK**

ADAM: The group chat. Be honest. What happens when everyone is making plans and you are the grey tick.

MARCUS: The grey tick is a personality now. I open it. I read the jokes. I type three replies and delete them because they sound like a person I am not currently employing. Then the landlord says, see, you don't belong in the bright room. I put the phone face down and call that a decision.

DEPRESSION: Face down is rest.

MARCUS: Face down is hiding. Rest would be a nap and a text that says I'm out tonight, love you. I can do the nap. The text is apparently an extreme sport.

ADAM: Send one while I'm here. Not a redemption scene. A Tuesday. I'll look at the kettle.

MARCUS: Fine. Can't tonight. Not personal. Have one for me. There. The landlord hated every word.

DEPRESSION: I hated tonight. It implies other nights. Optimistic. Reckless.

MARCUS: There will be other nights. Some of them I'll attend. You don't get to pre-grieve my calendar.

ADAM: What do we do with the grey tick from our side. And if you say pottery I am leaving.

MARCUS: Don't narrate my absence to the group. Marcus is being weird becomes a story I then have to re-enter. Say he's out, we'll see him. If I come back, don't do a welcome parade. A parade means I was gone in a way that needs forgiving. I needed a chair. That's different.

DEPRESSION: He was absent. Call it what it is.

MARCUS: I was absent from the pub. I was not absent from my life. You love collapsing those.

ADAM: One thing you still want that the landlord calls embarrassing.

MARCUS: To be invited even when I say no. The invitation is the proof the chair still exists. If people stop asking, the landlord gets to say I told you they were better off. Keep asking. Let me decline without a tribunal. That's the whole friendship. Ask. Allow no. Leave the chair.

DEPRESSION: They will get bored of asking.

MARCUS: Some will. The ones who don't are the house. You can sneer at them from the hallway. You don't get their keys.

ADAM: And if I am the one who stopped asking for a year.

MARCUS: Then start again without a speech about how you've grown. A message that says pub Thursday, no is fine, is worth more than an apology essay. I don't need you punished. I need the chair put back.

---

ADAM: Afterward. I invented normal reasons for Marcus for years. Work. Tired. Busy. The silence after I asked what the nothing felt like was the most honest answer he gave me. The landlord is still in the chair. Marcus is still in the house. Next time. Elena. And the security guard who never clocks off.

---

# EPISODE 3 — THE SECURITY GUARD WHO NEVER CLOCKS OFF
**Show:** The Other Person in the Room
**Host:** Adam James
**Guest:** Elena
**Third voice:** Trauma
**Runtime target:** 12–15 minutes
**Tone:** Kitchen. Hallway light on in daylight. No graphic events. Interrupt. Argue. The guard is loyal and exhausting.

---

**CONTENT NOTE**

ADAM: Content note. Trauma. Hypervigilance. Body memory. No graphic detail. If you need to leave, leave. I'm Adam James. This is The Other Person in the Room. Episode three. Elena.

ELENA: You left the hallway light on.

ADAM: It was already on. It's the middle of the day.

ELENA: And yet. Sit where I can see the door. Not behind me.

ADAM: Noted. Empty chair by the door. Sightlines. As specified by someone who has never once relaxed in a restaurant.

ELENA: I relax. I just do it facing the exit like a person with taste.

ADAM: Introduce the third person before it introduces itself.

---

**THE GUARD**

ELENA: A very tired security guard who never clocks off. Always standing. Always scanning. Convinced that if it sits down for one minute the original threat will walk back in wearing a different face. Loyal in the most exhausting way possible.

TRAUMA: I heard loyal. I'll take it.

ELENA: You missed exhausting.

TRAUMA: Exhausting is a review. Loyal is the job description.

ADAM: Does it ever sit.

TRAUMA: Sitting is how people get surprised. I don't do surprised.

ELENA: You don't do lunch either. Or friends. Or a film where someone closes a door too loudly.

TRAUMA: Loud doors are information.

ELENA: Loud doors are doors.

ADAM: Who were you before this had a job title.

ELENA: Louder. I danced in kitchens. I assumed tomorrow would be roughly the same shape as today. I thought danger announced itself, like a weather warning, with time to bring the washing in. I didn't know the body could keep a private archive and not tell the owner what the filing system was.

TRAUMA: The archive is the point. Forgetting is how it gets a second chance.

ELENA: Forgetting is also how people have birthdays.

**THE MUTINIES**

MEI: Alert. Exhausted. Holds a list of every possible wrong sentence and the precise social death that follows. It thinks it's keeping me safe from humiliation. It is succeeding at keeping me very small.

SOCIAL: Small is safe. Safe is the brief.

MEI: Safe is also missing. I would like to be in the photograph occasionally.

SOCIAL: Photographs are permanent evidence. I don't love evidence.

ADAM: What happens when you walk into a room with more than three people.

MEI: A continuous risk assessment. Who is bored. Who is kind. Which sentence would make them leave. The temperature changes and I feel it before I understand it. Silence is not coldness. Sometimes silence is the only way I can stay without combusting.

SOCIAL: Better to say nothing than to say the thing that makes them leave.

MEI: Better for who. You get a quiet room. I get a reputation for being chilly. I am not chilly. I am spending money to remain.

ADAM: I thought you were uninterested. For years.

MEI: I was interested and terrified, which from the outside is a very good impression of uninterested. Thank you for saying it. I can forgive it faster if you don't do a speech.

ADAM: No speech. I was wrong.

MEI: Good. Short. The thermostat hates short. Short doesn't give it anything to scan.

SOCIAL: Short replies are dangerous. They can be read twelve ways.

MEI: They can also be a person being kind on a bus. Liam and I should start a support group for hostile interpretations.

**THE COST**

ADAM: What does staying in the room actually cost.

MEI: Heat. Calculation. Holding a sentence in my mouth until it goes cold and then swallowing it. People think quiet is easy. Quiet is labour. If you sit next to it without filling it, that's the kind version. If you fill it because you're uncomfortable, I have to manage your discomfort as well as mine. I don't have the staff.

SOCIAL: Filling the quiet is how mistakes get in. I keep the temperature low enough that no one gets burned. Including her.

MEI: Including me by making sure I never touch anything. That's not thermostat behaviour. That's a museum.

ADAM: What did practice look like. No manual. Just the feel of it.

MEI: Repeatedly doing the thing that felt like dying until it only felt like severe discomfort. Ordering. Staying ten minutes longer. Saying one true sentence and not apologising for it. Progress is unglamorous. Nobody claps for a woman who stayed at a dinner until pudding. I clapped internally. The thermostat filed a complaint.

SOCIAL: Pudding is when people get careless. Careless is when they see you.

MEI: I would like to be seen and not evacuated. Those are compatible. You just refuse the compatibility.

ADAM: What should we do with the quiet.

MEI: Don't thank me like I've donated a kidney. A small nod is plenty. The quiet is me paying to stay, not a mystery for you to solve with a podcast voice. If you stay too, the bill splits. That's not a slogan. That's maths I can afford. Ease is the costume. Remaining is the friendship. And if you say "I love that you're opening up" I will move the table myself, into the car park.

SOCIAL: Car parks are also public.

MEI: Car parks have fewer speeches. I'm considering it.

**THE CONTRACT**

ADAM: Do you want it gone.

MEI: I want a lower setting. I don't want to become a person with no caution. Caution has saved me from a few real humiliations. I want caution that doesn't confiscate the evening. I want to say the sentence while it's still warm.

SOCIAL: Warm sentences are how people leave.

MEI: Cold silence is how people never arrive. I'm picking a third option. It's called talking, and it's very fashionable.

ADAM: Say the contract.

MEI: You can lower the temperature. You cannot set it to zero. You get a veto on the actually cruel sentence, not on every sentence. You don't get to narrate my quiet as mystery or rudeness. And if I stay, you mark it as a success, not as a risk you failed to prevent.

SOCIAL: I'll keep measuring.

MEI: Measure quietly. I'm going to say one more thing before we stop, and you don't get a note in the margin.

ADAM: What's the thing.

MEI: Thank you for moving the table. It was a small thing. It was also the difference between me doing this and me going home. Small is not the same as nothing.

ADAM: Do the party you left. I was there. I thought you'd gone to take a call.

MEI: I went to the hallway and negotiated with the thermostat about whether my joke had killed the room. It hadn't. Someone laughed. The laugh arrived half a second late and the thermostat called it pity. I stood by the coats like a woman waiting for a taxi out of her own evening. You found me and said you thought I was on the phone. I let you think it. The truth was too long for a hallway.

SOCIAL: The hallway was correct. The room was too hot.

MEI: The room was a birthday. Heat is not an attack. You treat every degree like a fire.

ADAM: What would have helped in that hallway. Specifically.

MEI: "You don't have to come back in, and you can if you want, and either way I'm getting cake." No analysis. No "you seem quiet." I know I seem quiet. It's my most expensive hobby. Offer the exit and the return in the same sentence so the scanner can't pick a tragedy. Then talk about the cake. Cake is a handrail.

SOCIAL: Cake is a distraction from risk.

MEI: Cake is a reason to stay. I'm building a personality out of reasons. You're not invited to the planning meeting.

ADAM: Who are you when the thermostat is on low. I want that person on tape, not only the labour.

MEI: Funny. A bit mean, in a loving way. Curious. I remember people's stories. I am good at the corner of a room and, on the right night, good in the middle too. The thermostat tells me that version is a risk. I think that version is the point of leaving the house. If you only meet the quiet, you met the invoice, not the friend.

SOCIAL: The friend gets hurt.

MEI: The friend also gets a life. I'm choosing the life. You can file a complaint in the corner.

ADAM: Work. Friends. Family. Which room costs the most, and don't say all of them unless you mean it.

MEI: Family costs the most because they knew the louder version and treat the quiet as a demotion. Work costs in meetings where speaking is mistaken for value. Friends cost less when they've learned the corner. The thermostat ranks them all as danger. I rank them as different prices. I can pay some of them. I can't pay all of them in one week. That's not snobbery. That's a budget.

SOCIAL: Budgets slip. Then they see you and they leave.

MEI: Some leave. Some stay and tell me the joke landed. I have evidence. You delete it. I'm keeping a folder called it landed. It's small. It's ruining your thesis.

ADAM: Say what you want from a room that actually wants you in it.

MEI: A corner if I need it, and no commentary when I take the middle. Someone who can tolerate the gap between my face and my sentence. An exit that isn't a rejection. And the right to be funny without it being filed as a risk. I am funny. I would like that on the record before the thermostat appends a disclaimer.

SOCIAL: Disclaimer: humour increases visibility.

MEI: Visibility is how friendship works. I'm opting in. You can minute your objection. The meeting is adjourned.

ADAM: Last instruction for a tired friend. One line they can actually remember.

MEI: Move the table if the middle is a stage. Don't narrate the quiet. Offer cake and an exit in the same breath. Believe that staying was the loud thing, even if I barely spoke. And if I am funny, don't look so relieved that it becomes a performance review. Laugh and stay. That's the whole manual. It fits on a napkin. The thermostat can have the rest of the paper.

SOCIAL: Napkins get lost.

MEI: So do evenings, if I let you run them. I'm keeping the napkin. Adam, that's the episode. Don't add a bow. Bows make it hot in here.

**THE MIDDLE OF THE ROOM**

ADAM: You said on the right night you can do the middle. Tell me about a right night, and then tell me what the thermostat tried to charge you for it.

MEI: A birthday. Not mine. I told the story about the terrible boat and people laughed for real, not the polite laugh that means please stop. I stayed in the middle for twenty minutes. The invoice arrived in the car home. Every sentence re-graded. A pause I took became proof I'd been boring. The laugh became proof I'd been too much. The thermostat billed me twice for opposite sins. That's the scam. There is no correct volume. There's only a bill.

SOCIAL: Both can be true. Too much and not enough is a known risk.

MEI: Both can be a racket. I was a person at a birthday. The boat story is funny. I have witnesses who aren't you. Their laughter counts. Your invoice doesn't.

ADAM: If I'm the friend who only ever meets the corner version. How do I stop confirming the bill.

MEI: Invite me, put us at the end of the table, and don't reward the quiet with relief. The relief is a tell. It says thank god you stayed small. If I start a story, don't look surprised. Surprise is a review. Just listen, then tell yours. Turn-taking is the whole cure. Not exposure therapy as a party trick. A conversation that assumes I have something to say.

SOCIAL: Assuming that is how embarrassment starts.

MEI: Assuming that is how friendship starts. Embarrassment is survivable. I've checked. The boat story survived. I survived. You're the only one still writing the incident report.

ADAM: Family. They knew the louder kid. What's the sentence you wish they'd retire.

MEI: "You used to be so outgoing." That's a eulogy for a volume, not a question about a person. Replace it with "how was your week" and then wait long enough for the answer to arrive in my accent, not theirs. If the answer is short, the week was still real. Don't fill it with the outgoing ghost. She's not dead. She's just not performing on command. There's a difference, and I would like it respected at Christmas.

SOCIAL: Command is safer. Spontaneity is a liability.

MEI: Christmas is already a liability. I'm bringing the boat story anyway. You can sit with the coats. The coats don't heckle.
